Blendering — turning a real property into a real render

This page is the field guide for producing accurate 3D renders of 160 Still Forest Dr. The render quality settings (Cycles, AgX Punchy, OIDN, 4K, 2048 samples) are already wired into blender/render_property.py. What's still missing is the real-world input: photo-derived materials, drainage paths, and measurements that aren't in the database. Everything below is how to capture that input and feed it into the system so Claude can stitch it into a render.

Priority order (biggest visual impact first): real terrain (LiDAR DEM) → photo-based PBR materials → drainage & water flow → manually modeled structures → vegetation → AgX/2048 samples polish.
De-prioritized: outdoor lighting setup. Nishita sky + a Poly Haven HDRI is good enough out of the box — don't spend time tuning sun angles or area lights. The render engine settings already encode reasonable defaults.

1. Photos → Materials (the highest-leverage workflow)

A material is what makes a wall look like stone vs. concrete vs. wood. The current script paints surfaces with flat RGB swatches, which is why the render looks like a zoning map. Real materials are PBR texture sets — usually 4 image maps that Blender combines: base color, roughness, normal, displacement.

The two paths from a photo to a usable material

AMatch-and-grab from a free library (90% of cases)

Take a close-up photo of the surface (corrugated container side, stone foundation, cedar siding). Then go find a visually similar CC0 PBR set on ambientCG or Poly Haven and tint the base color in Blender to match the actual photo. You're not photographing for the material — you're photographing to identify the right library set.

  • Photograph the surface flat-on, in even shade (not direct sun, no shadows from your body).
  • Frame about 2 ft × 2 ft of surface — enough to see the repeating pattern.
  • Note the dominant color in the photo (eyedropper in any image viewer works).
  • In ambientCG, search the category (e.g. "Metal Corrugated", "Wood Planks Painted") and pick one whose roughness/scale matches.
  • Download the 2K or 4K JPG bundle and drop into ~/Projects/alpacapps-3d/textures/AAP_category_name/
  • In Blender, multiply the base color with an RGB node tinted to your photo's color.
BPhoto-to-PBR (when nothing in the library matches)

For unique surfaces (the specific stone on the Main House foundation, a custom paint job), generate the PBR maps directly from your photo:

  • Materialize (free, GUI app, Mac/Win) — drop in one photo, auto-generates albedo + normal + roughness + height. Best for tileable surfaces.
  • Substance Sampler (paid, ~$20/mo) — best results, handles non-flat photos.
  • Image-to-PBR via Claude — take 4 photos of the surface from slightly different angles in flat light, drop them in property_data/textures_raw/, and ask Claude in this project to run the photo-to-PBR pipeline. Claude can invoke an Alpuca-side script that uses oidn-denoise + a normal-map estimator (e.g. NormalMapGenerator or a small ONNX model) and writes the result to ~/Projects/alpacapps-3d/textures/AAP_*.

Make it tileable: Whatever path you use, run the result through GIMP's Filters → Map → Tile / Make Seamless so the texture doesn't show grid lines when stretched across a wall.

Naming convention

All materials follow AAP_<category>_<name>. This matches what Claude expects when scanning the textures folder, and it keeps the Blender shader graph readable. 

~/Projects/alpacapps-3d/textures/
  AAP_structure_main_house_stone/
    base_color.jpg
    roughness.jpg
    normal.jpg
    displacement.jpg
    source_photos/        ← keep originals
    source.txt            ← link to ambientCG asset OR "captured on-site 2026-04-08"
  AAP_structure_red_container_corrugated/
    ...

2. Water drainage — capture & express

Water drainage is critical for this property: Bastrop sandy-clay soil, gentle slope, septic constraints, and the Main House sits in a shallow swale. The render must show where water actually goes during a heavy rain — not just terrain elevation contours.

What to capture on-site

ObservationHow to record it
Standing water spots after rainGPS pin + photo + rough size in feet. Use the iPhone Compass app for lat/lon.
Erosion channels / rillsWalk the channel, drop a GPS pin every ~10 ft. Photograph from above.
Roof downspout outletsPhoto of where each downspout discharges; note splash block / pipe / nothing.
Driveway runoff directionStand uphill, pour a water bottle, photograph the path it takes.
Septic drainfield perimeterGPS-pin the corners. This is a no-flow zone for designed drainage.
Culverts, swales, French drainsPin both ends + photo + estimated capacity (pipe diameter, swale width).

Schema — store it in PostGIS

Drainage features go in a new table so they can be queried, rendered, and audited the same way structures are. Claude creates this on request via the Supabase Management API: 

CREATE TABLE drainage_features (
  id SERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
  parcel_id INT REFERENCES parcels(id),
  feature_type TEXT,         -- 'flow_path', 'standing_water',
                             -- 'downspout', 'culvert', 'swale',
                             -- 'erosion_channel', 'septic_drainfield'
  geom GEOMETRY,             -- POINT, LINESTRING, or POLYGON in EPSG:4326
  flow_direction_deg INT,    -- 0-360, where water moves (for points)
  observed_during TEXT,      -- 'heavy_rain', 'normal', 'drought'
  width_ft NUMERIC,          -- for swales/channels
  depth_in NUMERIC,          -- for standing water / channels
  capacity_note TEXT,        -- "8in PVC", "12in corrugated"
  notes TEXT,
  photo_urls TEXT[],
  observed_at DATE,
  observed_by TEXT
);

How drainage shows up in the render

  • Flow paths (LINESTRINGs) become animated blue arrows on the terrain in the bird's-eye render — Claude bakes them as Geometry Nodes curves that ride the LiDAR DEM surface.
  • Standing water (POLYGONs) become slightly reflective puddle meshes draped on the terrain, using AAP_landscape_water_pool with displacement turned off.
  • Erosion channels get a darker dirt material (AAP_landscape_soil_bed) and a small displacement carve.
  • Septic drainfield renders as a translucent yellow overlay so it's visible but doesn't dominate.
  • Downspouts & culverts become small geometry markers with arrow icons showing flow direction.

The result is a render that doubles as a drainage diagram — useful for permit review, landscaping decisions, and explaining to a contractor why the swale on the west side has to stay clear.

3. Integrating inputs into the renderings pipeline

Once you have photos, materials, and drainage data captured, Claude wires them into the existing render pipeline. The flow is:

1Upload raw assets

Drop into structured folders on Alpuca (paca@192.168.1.200). Claude can rsync from your laptop or pull from a Supabase Storage bucket if you upload via the admin Media tab. 

~/Projects/alpacapps-3d/
  property_data.json          ← from fetch_property_data.py
  textures/                   ← AAP_* PBR sets
  reference_photos/
    structures/
      main_house_front.jpg
      main_house_back.jpg
      ...
    drainage/
      flow_path_west_swale_001.jpg
      ...
  drainage_geojson/           ← exports from PostGIS
  renders/v1/                 ← output PNGs
2Push drainage observations into PostGIS

Either edit a CSV and ask Claude to bulk-insert via the Management API, or just tell Claude in natural language: "There's a 6-foot-wide standing water spot 30 feet northwest of the Back House after heavy rain, about 4 inches deep." Claude geocodes against the parcel boundary and writes the row.

3Re-fetch & render

fetch_property_data.py already pulls structures from PostGIS. Claude extends it (or adds fetch_drainage_data.py) to also pull drainage_features, then runs: 

RENDER_PREVIEW=1 ssh paca@192.168.1.200 \
  '/Applications/Blender.app/Contents/MacOS/Blender -b \
   -P ~/Projects/alpacapps-3d/render_property.py'

Preview render is ~2 min on Alpuca M4. Drop RENDER_PREVIEW=1 for the 4K hero (~15–45 min).

4Publish to the Renderings tab

The final PNG is uploaded to Supabase Storage (housephotos/renders/<date>_<view>.jpg) and added to the RENDERINGS array in spaces/admin/phyprop.js. Push to main, GitHub Pages deploys, and the new render appears one tab to the left under Renderings.

4. How to talk to Claude to manage this

Most of the steps above are things you describe to Claude in this project rather than running by hand. Here are the prompt patterns that work:

Adding a material from a photo

I just uploaded a photo of the Main House foundation stone to
reference_photos/structures/main_house_foundation_detail.jpg.
Find a matching PBR set on ambientCG, download it to
textures/AAP_structure_main_house_stone/, tint the base color
to match the photo, and update render_property.py to use it.

Recording a drainage observation

After yesterday's rain there's standing water about 8 ft long
and 3 ft wide along the south edge of the gravel parking area,
roughly 2 inches deep. Add it to drainage_features and link the
photo I just uploaded to drainage/parking_standing_water_001.jpg.

Describing a flow path

Water from the Main House roof downspout on the NE corner runs
about 25 ft northeast across the lawn, then turns east into the
existing swale by the driveway. Add it as a flow_path linestring
in drainage_features.

Producing a new render

Re-fetch property_data, render the bird's-eye view at 4K with
the new drainage overlay turned on, and upload it to the
Renderings tab.

What Claude needs from you (the only manual bits)

  • Take the photos and upload them (or share them in chat).
  • Describe drainage observations in plain English with rough distances.
  • Approve material picks before they're committed (Claude shows the ambientCG asset URL + a swatch comparison).
  • Approve the final render before it's published to the Renderings tab.
