What actually happened

A guest booked 3 months (Sep 2 – Nov 30) and stayed 17 days. Around Sep 17, the local water utility flushed its lines and the water was discolored for about 3 days. The utility's published guidance says this is sediment, it's temporary, and running the cold tap clears it. There was no boil-water notice. The property has a 6-stage filtration system for drinking water and 3 other filters.

On Sep 19 the guest messaged: "I'm leaving today. Monday's payment won't go through. I've requested the unused portion through Airbnb." Six and a half hours later, he asked for the first time whether the water was safe. It was. I live about 10 feet from the unit, and the day before he'd told me everything was great.

The three real failures

  • 1. Priority doesn't change, no matter how much harm Airbnb causes. Airbnb admits it took money from a host against its own Senior Case Manager's ruling. That case waits in the same queue, in the same order, as any routine question. The phone rep couldn't escalate it, couldn't reach a supervisor, and couldn't move it up. How badly Airbnb hurt the host has no effect on how fast Airbnb fixes it. That isn't a staffing gap. It's a design decision.
  • 2. No timely response. Two days of silence in the case thread. On the phone: "very soon," then "eventually," then "it could take a month." No date, and no maximum.
  • 3. The case went to someone who was out for a week. Nobody noticed. Nothing happened until the system automatically unassigned it.

Failures 2 and 3 are operational problems, and any company can fix those. Failure 1 is a choice.

Key evidence

This isn't hard to fix

  • Put cases Airbnb admits it got wrong at the top of the queue.
  • Don't assign cases to people who are out.
  • Use AI for two basic checks: Were the host's questions actually answered? Does what the agent said match Airbnb's published policy? A basic model does this reliably, and it would have caught this case on day 1.
